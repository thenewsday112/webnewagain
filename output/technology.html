I built a robot to push back against delivery bots and bring art to the streets

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
A pink, otherworldly moving sculpture made of wood and foam is now facing off against delivery robots on the streets of Philadelphia as a form of public art.

Courtesy of Madison Polidoro

  • Madison Polidoro built a remote-controlled robot to rival AI-powered delivery bots.
  • Polidoro made the sculpture by hand to bring human-made art and curiosities back to the streets.
  • The creature, named Awebot, now roams city streets and occasionally faces off with delivery robots.

This as-told-to essay is based on a conversation with Madison Polidoro, a fine artist and fabricator based in Philadelphia. She collaborated with the art collective Ministry of Awe to create a remote-controlled robot in response to the delivery robots on the streets of her city. It has been edited for length and clarity.

The first time I saw a food delivery robot, I stopped dead in my tracks.

I didn't know what this thing was. I still don't fully understand it. All I knew was that it seemed to be looking at me and following me down the street, and it freaked me out.

So it was really exciting to think about making something I could put out on the street to rival them. I'm a maker who makes everything by hand — no AI, not even 3D modeling — mostly pen and paper.

The result is a moving artwork I named Orifice, which then became known as the Awebot. My sculpture loves people, but she's born to be a menace to the delivery robot ecosystem.

I wanted to bring my world of creatures into the street

I've been a sculptor since childhood and a fabricator for a little over a decade. I'm constantly adding to my little world of creatures.

Around 2020, I started working with Ministry of Awe, an art collective creating an immersive installation. Through their commissions, I've been able to execute more ambitious ideas, work on a larger scale, and integrate more expensive features.

They asked me to make something mobile that they could take into the streets. Originally, we were thinking big, perhaps some kind of float. We wanted to bring the art to the people.

Around that same time, I started seeing food delivery robots in Philadelphia. Then they became a more regular occurrence, especially around the collective.

At our Tuesday co-creation nights, we agreed it would make more sense to shrink the sculpture into something resembling those robots. They were this freaky, weird thing on everyone's mind.

As an artist and fabricator, I feel the pressure to work faster and faster. The advancements in tech and AI come with these weird, uncanny feelings that intensify everything, and not in a good way. My art already plays on emotional discomfort, so it was perfectly aligned.

I YouTubed my way through building her

This was my first tech-integrated project. I'm not tech-savvy by any means, so I really just YouTubed my way through it.

We ordered the robotic base, including the wheels and motor. On top of that, I built an aluminum frame to house the internal parts and create a cavity for the functional butthole component. You could theoretically use it for food delivery, although that isn't the intention. You could put anything in there.

I built another frame for the skin layer, added wood and foam, carved the foam down, and sculpted onto it. Then I coated and painted it, creating this weather-resistant, plasticky, rubbery creature.

There are silicone components, including the tongue and orifice. I also put my friend Nina's hair in there. All the hair in my art is real. Friends give it to me, or people who follow me on Instagram mail it to me.

For me, it's a ritual to breathe life and humanness into them.

She's a predator, but people seem to love her

Awebot's eyes and wheels are operated by remote control. The collective has an employee who takes her out and drives her around.

It's a really fun improv experience. What happens depends on the public. Some people have a whole interaction with her. Kids come up to her. Dogs come up to her. People take photos.

Sometimes we see delivery robots and have them face off. That has happened a couple of times. To me, they seem to treat her as an obstacle and as a threat, trying very hard to dodge her and get away.

I wanted to take this recognizable piece of technology and make something approachable that people would want to interact with. Something curious, fun, and silly.

The collective's concept is a bank where human emotion and imagination are the currency. Everything is handmade. You can see it and feel it. I think this sculpture captures that: taking ideas from this increasingly capitalized world and dialing them back into our humanity and creative needs.

I designed her with plans to continue the project. She may soon see siblings.

I graduated from college debt-free because I earned a full-ride scholarship. The benefits weren't just financial.

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
The author earned a full-ride scholarship to college.

Courtesy of Kelly Strul

  • I was offered a full-ride scholarship to Florida Atlantic University, and I accepted.
  • Not having to worry about money helped me explore my passion for dentistry and build a community.
  • I graduated debt-free, and now I'm going to dental school.

When my phone rang during my senior year of high school, it was Florida Atlantic University calling. I didn't know that answering would change the trajectory of my life.

I had 48 hours to decide whether to take a chance on an opportunity that seemed too good to be true: the opportunity to graduate debt-free.

After a last-minute decision to apply, I was admitted to Florida Atlantic University's Kelly/Strul Emerging Scholars Program, which provides a fully funded, four-year scholarship package.

The moment I received the call, a weight was lifted from both my family and me. College was no longer something my parents and I had to approach with fear about how we would afford it. The people who had spent years sacrificing to give me opportunities could finally watch me step into one without wondering what it would cost our family.

For me, the scholarship meant possibility. For my parents, it meant relief.

I didn't know what I wanted to major in, and some of my dreams still felt too ambitious to seriously pursue. But for the first time, I had the freedom to discover what I was capable of without letting difficult financial circumstances make that decision for me.

So, 48 hours after receiving that phone call, I said yes.

I almost let fear pick my major

When I first arrived at Florida Atlantic University, I didn't declare a major. I was considering studying accounting because numbers came naturally to me, and it seemed like a safe and successful career path.

There was just one problem. I had no passion for it.

I kept getting asked the same question: Why choose what's safe over what I actually wanted? It took time and a lot of encouragement before I realized the answer started with believing I could become more.

Having a full scholarship also changed what felt possible. Without the constant worry of how I would afford my education, I finally gave myself permission to explore the career I had once been too afraid to seriously pursue.

Shadowing a dentist changed everything

I began researching what it actually took to become a dentist and spent the following summer shadowing one. I expected the experience to help me understand the profession, but it did more than that. I fell in love with dentistry.

Even though I was spending hours observing without pay, it never felt like unpaid work. I was fascinated by the combination of science, precision, problem-solving, and human connection involved in dentistry. For the first time, I could clearly picture myself building a career around something that genuinely excited me, and I changed my major to biological sciences.

The financial support from the Kelly/Strul program made that transition possible in ways that went far beyond tuition. I didn't have to rush from class to work every day or take out loans simply to make ends meet. Instead, I had the freedom to study, explore my interests, become involved on campus, and figure out what kind of professional and person I wanted to become.

The scholarship gave me more than financial freedom. It gave me room to grow.

My biggest challenge reminded me of why community mattered

One of the most difficult periods of college came when I began studying for the Dental Admission Test while balancing coursework, leadership positions, and extracurricular commitments.

Without the stress of paying for tuition, housing, and meals, I was able to stay invested in the responsibilities and communities that had shaped my college experience instead of sacrificing them because of financial pressure.

But that season also taught me the importance of community. A men's group I had helped create to encourage and mentor others became the greatest source of encouragement for me. When I needed support, the same people I hoped to uplift were there to uplift me.

Sometimes, the greatest evidence that you have built something meaningful is seeing it carry you when you need help yourself.

I graduated debt-free and headed to dental school

Three years after answering that phone call, I graduated from Florida Atlantic University with my bachelor's degree in biological sciences.

As a first-generation student, I had accomplished something I once struggled to imagine for myself. I graduated in three years, debt-free, and prepared to begin dental school. But the greatest gift of the Kelly/Strul Emerging Scholars Program was not simply leaving college without student debt. It was leaving with a clearer understanding of who I was and what I was capable of becoming.

I entered Florida Atlantic uncertain about my major and hesitant to chase my dreams. I left with a degree, a community, leadership experience, and the confidence to pursue dentistry.

When I received that phone call almost four years ago, I thought I was simply saying yes to a scholarship, but I was really saying yes to a version of myself I hadn't met yet.

Another chapter of my life is calling as I begin dental school. This time, I don't need 48 hours to respond.

Ukraine's war robots keep soldiers out of danger, but humans still risk their lives to keep them running

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Ukraine's ground robots are designed to replace humans in the most dangerous parts of the battlefield, but humans still need to be in some risky areas to keep them running.

Dmytro Smolienko/ Ukrinform/Future Publishing via Getty Images

  • Ukraine's growing fleet of war robots is taking on dangerous missions once performed by soldiers.
  • But soldiers are still needed at the front to replace batteries and ammunition and make repairs.
  • Ukraine is, however, improving robot autonomy and range.

Ukraine is using a growing fleet of war robots to take dangerous roles away from humans, but soldiers sometimes still need to risk their lives to keep the machines running.

Ukraine wants to replace as many human soldiers in dangerous missions and roles as possible with robots and drones, and the technology has advanced enough that operators can now control some war robots from tens of kilometers away, according to Ukrainian brigades.

That can keep operators away from Russian fire, but while the robots, known as uncrewed ground vehicles, or UGVs, can replace soldiers on dangerous missions, other troops may still need to venture closer to the fighting to prepare, launch, maintain, and recover them.

For Ukraine's robotic missions, "at present, human involvement is both mandatory and indispensable," Oleksandr Yabchanka, head of the robotic systems for Ukraine's Da Vinci Wolves Battalion, told Business Insider.

"Someone must swap out batteries, perform maintenance, and troubleshoot technical issues," he said. Keeping a robot functional can mean replacing damaged parts, fixing punctured tires, and reloading ammunition.

Ukraine's ground robots replace humans in some missions by firing weapons, launching attacks, gathering intel, and carrying gear.

Diego Herrera Carcedo/Anadolu via Getty Images

The robots are typically designed to be relatively cheap and expendable, limiting the cost of losing one. Because their purpose is primarily to protect soldiers, units may decide that a damaged robot isn't worth risking a life to recover, but human support remains essential to keeping the fleet operational.

Many robots are designed to be reconfigured for different missions, allowing soldiers to swap equipment rather than use a separate machine for every task.

David Roberts, the CEO of ARX Robotics UK, the British arm of the German defense technology company of the same name, told Business Insider that the company's robots, some of which are in Ukraine, are designed so that it takes "literally seconds" to swap between a logistics setup, a communications payload, or whatever troops want to use it for.

That flexibility makes it easier to switch missions, but soldiers still need to be close enough to the front lines to reconfigure the machines.

Supporting soldiers will try to stay as far back from the fighting as they can, but factors like robots' range limit what's possible. Yabchanka said that "our philosophy is, above all, to preserve our personnel. Therefore, we strive to position our fighters as far as possible from the line of contact and the zones of highest risk—the so-called 'kill zones.'"

This applies in particular to operators and specialists in electronic warfare. "The exact distance to which they can be moved back depends on specific conditions and technical capabilities, but the general direction is precisely this."

Soldiers have to prepare and repair robots, and that sometimes has to be done in dangerous locations.

Ukrinform/NurPhoto via Getty Images

Some companies in Ukraine also send their staff to areas near the fighting to keep robots running. Ukrainian UGV maker DevDroid says robots "operate in extreme conditions — dirt, overheating, impacts, and electronic warfare. After several missions, they require diagnostics, updates, or repairs." It sends mobile teams out to units, including to front-line regions, to keep robots working.

Another challenge is that "the autonomy of UGVs is still in the early stages of development," Yabchanka said.

Though there have been technological breakthroughs, most of the ground robots still rely heavily on remote control rather than navigating and completing missions independently. That can leave them stranded if something goes wrong.

Their dependence on continuous communications can also limit how safely operators can position themselves on the battlefield.

There is a similar dynamic in play with aerial drones. Ukraine is developing technology that allows pilots to stay much farther from danger, but support crews still need to work closer to the fighting. Some drone operators can control their drones from relatively safe locations hundreds or even thousands of miles away, but someone still has to prepare, arm, and launch the aircraft in the area where it's needed.

Satellite communications technology means units are "able to put our pilots in any place when there is a stable internet connection for them," Dmytro "Liber" Zhluktenko, a former drone operator who now serves as a lessons-learned analyst with Ukraine's 413th Unmanned Systems Regiment "RAID," told Business Insider.

"But this doesn't remove the need for the ground groups, people who actually attach the explosive, put the drone outside of the bunker, et cetera," he said.

Ukraine is working to reduce those risks by improving the range, reliability, and off-road mobility of its robots, as well as connectivity solutions, allowing support crews to operate farther from the front.

Operators being able to stay far from their robots means that if the robots are destroyed, they are safe.

Ukrinform/NurPhoto via Getty Images

Ukraine is also increasingly using ground robots to recover other ground robots rather than sending human crews, and companies are designing parts and batteries that can be replaced more quickly to shorten the time soldiers spend servicing them in the field.

Greater autonomy is another priority, potentially allowing robots to operate with less human control. An uncrewed systems unit announced this week that a robot had successfully completed a 12-mile supply run almost entirely on its own. The unit said the mission was the "first of its kind under combat conditions."

Mykyta Rozhkov, the chief business development officer for Ukrainian defense tech company Frontline Robotics, told Business Insider that "right now more and more systems are getting autonomous features."

Yabchanka said the future of drones and robots "lies in the development of autonomy. Therefore, we need software solutions that will enable these systems to perform more functions independently, thereby reducing their reliance on a continuous connection with an operator."

Despite their limitations, robots are helping keep Ukraine's soldiers from having to do some of the battlefield's most dangerous jobs themselves.

Yabchanka said that "ground-based robotic systems are becoming an integral part of combat operations for most units deployed along the line of contact."

Anthropic's IPO push includes a big bet on life sciences

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Vas Narasimhan, CEO of the pharma giant Novartis, joined Anthropic's board of directors in April.

Hollie Adams/Bloomberg via Getty Images

  • Anthropic is starting to think like a drugmaker.
  • Anthropic's IPO filing outlines its ambitious plans for biological science.
  • AI in scientific research would aid Anthropic's mission and boost adoption of its Claude models.

Anthropic conquered coding. Now it's setting its sights on the human body.

Anthropic's S-1 filing, which outlines its ambitions and financials ahead of a massive initial public offering next month, reads as a life-sciences manifesto. The maker of Claude sees itself as a crucial cog in the next generation of biological and medicinal research — and is selling that lucrative vision to its would-be investors.

Anthropic is hoping for a wave of new discoveries that will support its do-gooder mission and convince the public of AI's power and worth. Its life-science focus could also support financial growth beyond its AI software engineering tools. It would promote the use of Anthropic's most advanced, expensive models in an industry already accustomed to spending billions on research and development.

The S-1 filing is scattered with references to biotech ambition. Its second line says AI "could rapidly accelerate scientific innovation" and "bring cures for major diseases within reach." A page later, biological progress tops the company's list of ways it hopes AI will improve lives.

Anthropic also references its life sciences business in its "growth strategy" section and writes that nine of the 10 largest pharmaceutical companies and eight of the 10 largest healthcare companies in the Fortune 500 spent at least $100,000 with Anthropic over a recent yearlong period.

"In the life sciences sector, where annual global pharmaceutical spend is expected to exceed $2 trillion in 2026, Claude is used in drug discovery and development by our customers, our biotechnology partners, and our own researchers," the company writes in the filing.

Anthropic declined to comment.

Anthropic has begun investing in life sciences

Anthropic has already gotten to work investing in life sciences. The company's Claude Science product, which is in beta, came out in June. OpenAI, Anthropic's erstwhile rival, has a competing offering dubbed Rosalind, while Google spinoff Isomorphic Labs plans to use AI to create cancer-beating drugs — promising a struggle between companies for research customers.

Besides that, Anthropic started a molecular biology lab in the Bay Area this year and spent $400 million in stock in April to buy the biotech startup Coefficient Bio. Vas Narasimhan, the CEO of the pharmaceutical company Novartis, also joined Anthropic's board in April. In August, Anthropic announced a new robotics standard that allows AI to control lab equipment.

The company's careers page shows it's on a hiring spree for life sciences roles across chemistry, experimental biology, research engineering, and more. Two new roles opened late this week, offering $320,000 salaries to researchers capable of designing and manufacturing antibodies.

In its S-1, Anthropic touted its work with pharma giant Lilly, saying the $1.3 trillion company has 50,000 employees with access to a Claude enterprise account and that more than 85% of its staff actively use the tool.

CEO Dario Amodei is prioritizing life sciences

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, a former theoretical physicist, has repeatedly touted AI's potential in the life sciences as a reason to advance the tech's capabilities.

In a September essay about "pacing" AI research, Amodei said he had survived a cancer that wouldn't have been treatable in the 1970s, and his father had died of a disease cured shortly after his death.

He previously elaborated on the themes in a 2024 essay dubbed "Machines of Loving Grace."

"Biology is probably the area where scientific progress has the greatest potential to directly and unambiguously improve the quality of human life," Amodei wrote, adding that he hopes AI-enabled research will help prevent and treat all infectious diseases, eliminate most cancers, and double the human lifespan.

The general public is less optimistic. Anthropic recently published survey results from August, finding that 91% of staff think AI will make a Nobel-level discovery by 2030, compared with 39% of US adults. No AI-designed drugs have ever received FDA approval.

Ben Mann, the co-leader of Anthropic's Labs organization, which builds new products and created Claude Code, told Business Insider this August that his small team is also focusing more on life sciences.

"Labs itself has shifted its focus from sort of more standard software engineering things to things like what Dario has described as curing all diseases like, Machines of Loving Grace-style," Mann said.

Google spinoff Isomorphic Labs is in a race to shake up the long, painful road to cancer-beating drugs

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Isomorphic Labs president Max Jaderberg believes that in five years' time, all drugs will be designed by AI.

Jillian Edelstein for BI

In a cavernous office in London's AI hub, a group of researchers is preparing to do battle with humanity's oldest enemy.

Isomorphic Labs, a spinoff of Google DeepMind and the brainchild of AI pioneer Demis Hassabis, has an ambitious mission even by the standards of other AI labs: using AI to create drugs that could cure cancer and make disease a thing of the past.

Max Jaderberg, Isomorphic's president and the person running day-to-day operations at the company under Hassabis' aegis as CEO, knows it's a lofty goal. Even so, he is unfazed.

"It's a ridiculous statement, but we really say it with a straight face," said Jaderberg, 36, who spoke to Business Insider in an exclusive interview in September. He added that the rate at which Isomorphic employees were coming to him with internal breakthroughs had accelerated from monthly to weekly in the past three years.

"There's no world in five years' time, where all drugs aren't designed by AI," he said.

Right now, that goal seems a long way away. AI-designed drugs have been in development for over a decade, but none have yet received FDA approval or completed the clinical trials process, a yearslong regulatory slog that around 90% of drug candidates fail.

Isomorphic has enjoyed an early-mover advantage thanks to AlphaFold, the AI model developed by DeepMind that won Hassabis a Nobel Prize and revolutionized protein mapping, opening the door to AI-designed medicines.

Jaderberg said Isomorphic planned to use its AI models to overcome many of the hurdles that typically trip up promising drug candidates. The company is working on a method of using its models — and its plentiful supply of compute — to predict how the drug molecule will interact with every other molecule in the body, a process that would be far too expensive to test by hand.

In February, Isomorphic unveiled a "drug design engine" that the company said improves on AlphaFold.

Isomorphic plans to use it to precisely engineer molecules so that patients can receive the minimum amount of drug needed, Jaderberg said. The hope is that this will avoid complications caused by high concentrations of the drug building up in their bloodstream.

The company's ultimate goal, Jaderberg said, is to be able to predict the interaction between molecules so well that its scientists will not even have to step inside a lab while designing a drug.

Jaderberg said the company's internal drug design efforts are focused on developing breakthrough drugs for cancer, conditions related to the immune system, and inflammatory diseases.

"There's a lot of companies that just say, 'Here's a medicine in the clinic, can we just make a small change and make an incremental improvement that will allow us to capture some of that market?'" he said.

"I think we see a bigger opportunity, which is to go after medicines that haven't been made before," Jaderberg added.

From prototypes to patients

Jaderberg, who was part of Isomorphic's founding team, was recruited by Hassabis to DeepMind straight out of university. He initially approached Hassabis to be an angel investor in the computer vision startup he founded as a student, but Hassabis instead offered to buy the company outright to bring Jaderberg to DeepMind.

University of Oxford graduate Max Jaderberg became president of Isomorphic Labs in January.

Jillian Edelstein for BI

A former engineering student from a family of cancer researchers and doctors (his grandfather ran drug trials as a cardiologist in Sweden), Jaderberg was elevated to Isomorphic's president in January, having previously served as chief AI officer.

Now, he faces the challenge of actually shepherding Isomorphic's AI-designed drugs from prototypes to patients.

So far, Isomorphic has struck research partnerships with pharma giants Novartis, Johnson & Johnson, and Eli Lilly. Jaderberg said the AI startup's new drug design engine has also provided a foundation for the company to begin testing AI-designed drugs in preclinical trials, with human trials not far away.

"If we want to solve all disease, you need a drug design engine that doesn't just work for one disease area, one therapeutic area, one indication, one type of protein, even one modality. We need something that works for everything," said Jaderberg.

Isomorphic's president declined to provide a timeline for clinical trials, which the company has previously pushed back. However, Jaderberg said the company had not experienced any new delays and had been building up a clinical development team in Boston over the past year in preparation for the milestone.

Even with cutting-edge AI models and a healthy supply of compute, the barrier Isomorphic faces is daunting. Navigating the clinical trials gauntlet and taking drugs from concepts on a computer screen to real-life medicines is one of the most grueling challenges in biochemistry.

Researchers build molecules designed to target a specific protein, then subject them to an exhaustive trial-and-error process. Teams often end up going back to the drawing board when the drug fails to work on living cells as predicted or causes unexpected side effects.

Eventually, a few designs reach the stage where they can be tested on animals. After years of testing, a tiny selection might advance to human trials.

The entire process can take over a decade. Successful drug researchers can see just one or two of their drugs make it to patients across their entire career. Many more will have none.

Running the drug trial gauntlet

Jaderberg said there were a few reasons for the 90% failure rate drug candidates face.

Traditional biotechs are often funding-constrained, he said, so they have to take a chance on molecules they know are not perfect. The drug molecule might hit "off-target" proteins that can cause unintended side effects and toxicity.

In Jaderberg's view, however, the underlying reasons for failure run deeper. Unsuccessful drug candidates usually fail because they are based on an incorrect understanding of biology, he said.

Building a truly revolutionary drug design engine requires a comprehensive model of biology that can understand how proteins interact, and the step-by-step chemical reactions that dictate how cells grow and die, he said.

"I think that points to a deeper and longer-term research goal that we're already underway on. Just how can you understand biology mechanistically better?" said Jaderberg.

Despite Isomorphic's ambition, the road to the first AI-designed cancer drugs is still uncertain.

AI-first drug design startups like Recursion and Schrödinger have reported setbacks for their AI-designed medications, and some high-profile drug candidates have failed to move beyond Phase 1 trials.

Experts have urged caution. In a March essay, Emilia Javorsky, a director at the Future of Life Institute, warned that while AI excels at solving problems that are rules-based or data-rich, biology is neither. Javorsky criticized "flippant promises by tech CEOs" that their technologies will quickly usher in treatments for diseases that have tormented humanity for millennia.

Lennard Lee, an associate professor at the University of Oxford and project lead for the UK's cancer vaccine AI & supercomputing project, told Business Insider that AI has transformed scientists' ability to predict molecular properties and design new medicines. However, he warned that these medicines still face their "ultimate test."

"Drug development remains extraordinarily difficult, and promising predictions frequently fail when confronted with biological reality," Lee said.

Charlotte Deane, a professor of structural bioinformatics at the University of Oxford, said that many proteins within the body have a fluid structure that is hard for even the most advanced AI tools to model.

"We all aim for the stars and that's exactly what we should be doing. But it is still a long process to show that a drug works," Deane said.

'A SpaceX for drug discovery'

The success of AlphaFold may have given Isomorphic an early lead in the race to discover cancer-beating drugs, but both Anthropic and OpenAI have unveiled their own drug-discovery efforts in recent months.

In April, OpenAI announced GPT‑Rosalind, a dedicated frontier AI model built to shorten the time from drug discovery to approval, and announced partnerships with pharma giants Novo Nordisk and Moderna. In October, the ChatGPT maker said it had acquired AI-powered drug development platform Orchestra Bio for an undisclosed sum.

Anthropic, which recently revealed plans for a blockbuster IPO, said this month it had partnered with six life sciences organizations to deploy AI throughout drug development and accelerate the clinical trial process. The AI lab has also set up its own internal drug discovery efforts that are targeting treatments for diseases, including rare childhood forms of muscular dystrophy, with Anthropic aiming to begin human trials as early as late 2028.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei made his belief that AI could cure most major diseases in the next five to 10 years the opening salvo of his call to pace the frontier last month, revealing that he had survived an "early-stage cancer" himself.

In an X post in August, Amodei went further. He suggested that delivering real progress in the fight against diseases like cancer would be necessary to help the AI labs turn around the increasingly skeptical public view of the AI boom and justify the enormous cost in money and land.

"At this point, saying that AI will cure cancer is more a cliche than it is inspiring, and most people think it is deceptive. The thing that will work is actually curing cancer," Amodei wrote.

Jaderberg argued that top AI scientists are thinking more about "impact" when deciding where to work.

Jillian Edelstein for BI

Jaderberg said that frontier labs like Anthropic and OpenAI, both of which are eyeing massive public listings, are more interested in "building up hype" and communicating results than Isomorphic was.

"We don't want to over hype what we're doing. We're not trying to sell software. We're not trying to license our models. We want to make medicines for patients," he added.

He said he ultimately envisioned Isomorphic as a vertically integrated drugmaker that designs, builds, and ships its medicines directly to patients; a "SpaceX for drug discovery."

'We have a ton of Demis time'

Isomorphic boasts one huge advantage over its rivals: Hassabis, who remains the company's CEO after stepping away from his role as head of DeepMind in August to take on a broader research role at Alphabet.

In his post announcing his new role, Hassabis said he would be "leaning into" his work at Isomorphic "to help cure disease." Jaderberg said there had been no change in Isomorphic's organizational structure, but added that the company was already benefiting from Hassabis' new focus.

"He believes this is one of the most important missions we should be pushing. We have a ton of Demis time already to help push us on that vision, and having even more of his headspace is going to be positive for us," Jaderberg said.

Jaderberg added that he expects Isomorphic's singular goal to give it another advantage over Anthropic and OpenAI in the most hotly-contested part of the AI business: hiring.

Both Anthropic and OpenAI have been rocked by high-profile departures in recent months. Researchers from both companies have quit with dramatic warnings of catastrophic safety risks and AI-driven apocalypses.

Speaking before those departures, Jaderberg said that Isomorphic offered researchers a route to apply AI in a way that would have a positive real-world impact.

"I think there are a lot of AI scientists and engineers who actually want to see that final impact and want it to be something really tangible and different to what I think is being offered as impact from the current frontier labs," Jaderberg said.

"As a species, we have to be driving this technology toward the good applications as well," he added.

Rivian needs R2 to work. I tested it to see if it's good enough.

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ben Shimkus/Business Insider

  • I drove the $61,485 Performance trim, but cheaper versions may be the sweet spot.
  • The R2 pairs goosebump-inducing speed with Rivian's playful personality.

I was walking away from the Rivian R2 when it gave me an audible confirmation that the doors had locked — but with a twist.

Instead of the typical, ugly horn blurt, it played a familiar jingle: the "buh BAH duh bah dah" instrumental riff in the "Ghostbusters" theme song.

"Oh my god, that's awesome," my dad, a giant "Ghostbusters" fan since the blockbuster movie came out in 1984, said over a FaceTime call. "Go back and play it again!"

I did. I think he heard the jingle five times on that call.

The R2 is Rivian's big bet that the EV-maker is about to go viral. It's the company's third consumer vehicle, following the larger and much more expensive R1S three-row SUV and R1T pickup.

By contrast, the R2 hits two American car market sweet spots: its base price will eventually start at $44,990 (a few thousand dollars below America's new-vehicle price average), and it's sized similarly to the ever-popular Toyota RAV4.

So I drove the hotly anticipated EV about 200 miles through southern New York State to see whether it feels good enough to carry that burden.

My top-end $61,485 Performance tester was a hoot — but prospective buyers would likely be better off exercising some patience.

Injecting some blockiness into an egg-shaped segment

The R2 adds some design pizzazz to the EV SUV segment.

Ben Shimkus/Business Insider

The R2's blockier design approach might matter more than it seems.

Most midsize EVs are slippery and egg-shaped, including America's EV bestseller, the Tesla Model Y.

But look at the non-EV market. Gas-powered SUVs with similarly swept-back proportions have generally remained niche products, often sold as lower-volume offshoots of more conventional SUVs.

Take, for example, BMW's lineup. Last year, the luxury manufacturer sold 76,546 units of the X3, its upright compact SUV. Its coupe-shaped equivalent, the X4, sold just 5,910 and was discontinued for the 2026 model year.

Rivian CEO RJ Scaringe has repeatedly said the EV market needs more choices around the $50,000 mark, arguing that buyers have had to compromise on several issues, like aesthetics.

The R2's secret weapon may be its shape.

Ben Shimkus/Business Insider

The R2 gives buyers something much closer to the upright SUV shape that dominates American sales — like the RAV4, Honda CR-V, or Nissan Rogue — without letting that form factor get boring.

Rivian carries over the stadium-shaped headlights made popular on the R1 series, giving its smaller SUV an instantly recognizable face. Inside, there are plenty of Rivian-isms, too: a huge glass roof, "Rivian" plates stitched into the seatbacks, and the flashlight hidden in the driver's side door panel.

I wouldn't buy the fastest R2

Rivian's well-laid-out interior gives me a ton of excitement for its cheaper models.

Ben Shimkus/Business Insider

As you've probably guessed, my Performance tester was fast.

The SUV — which gets power from its dual-motor setup — gets a silly amount of oomph for highway merges. That's hardly a surprise for Rivian, which gave its first generation of full-size EVs a dose of sports car urgency.

What surprised me was how much I wanted the R2 to calm down.

My favorite setting was Conserve, which softened the suspension and took some edge off the acceleration. In other words, my favorite version of the $61,485 Performance model was the one that made it act a little less like a Performance model.

The back seat was sun-soaked and comfy. The suspension, though, almost had my friends wearing their coffee.

Ben Shimkus/Business Insider.

My biggest complaint is the suspension.

At one point, I stopped for coffee with two other adults in the car. The passenger in the back seat was so worried about spilling his drink over a bump that he waited until we stopped again to take a sip.

That wasn't enough to ruin the car — but it reinforced my suspicion that many buyers might want to skip paying Cadillac-level prices for the R2.

Rivian knows how to have fun

The original Rivian R1s didn't get a glovebox. Rivian heard the complaints and added two glove boxes in the new generation to make up for it.

Ben Shimkus/Business Insider.

At its heart, Rivian is a fun-loving, techie brand — and the R2 captures that energy.

You can tell the car company has spent a lot of time thinking about its own software, which has improved since I last tested the R1S over a year ago. Menus are clearer, information is easier to read at highway speeds, and the R2's two haptic scroll wheels on the steering wheel felt weird for about an hour before becoming second nature.

(Though I still want a physical tab for adjusting the air vents.)

The R2 also gets the boring family-car stuff right. There's storage seemingly everywhere — two glove boxes, a deep center console, a small frunk, and a large rear cargo area with additional space under the floor. Over seven hours of driving, mine averaged 3.4 miles per kWh.

Then there are the completely unnecessary things.

Rivian has turned Halloween software updates into a tradition. This year's was "Ghostbusters"-themed, complete with green "slime" lighting, that lock jingle my dad loved, and a launch event where I somehow ended up meeting Bill Murray.

Sorry for the flex: Here's proof that I went to Rivian's Halloween patch launch and met Bill Murray.

Ben Shimkus/Business Insider

When Halloween ends, the theme disappears, and the R2 returns to its standard lock sound: naturally, it's birds chirping.

Those are the little flourishes that Rivian hopes will set its blocky SUV apart.

After driving the $61,485 version, I'm convinced Rivian has a compelling mass-market product. I just think the versions that matter most are still coming.
